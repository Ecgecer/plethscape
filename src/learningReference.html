Explore how the body shapes a PPG waveform

Plethscape is a free, interactive photoplethysmography (PPG) simulator. Select a wearable location on a 3D anatomical model, watch its simulated pulse waveform, and change one physiological setting at a time. Start at the wrist, then compare the finger, ear, temple, neck, upper arm and toe.

What is a PPG signal?

Photoplethysmography uses light to sense changes in blood volume in tissue. Its pulsatile waveform contains information about the timing and shape of each peripheral pulse. Plethscape makes these ideas visible alongside a beating heart, breathing lungs and an anatomical vascular network.

Why compare wearable locations?

The same heartbeat can produce different optical signals at different sites. Local perfusion, tissue properties, sensor contact and movement all matter. Use the location selector to compare the model's pulse contours while keeping the other settings fixed.

Which location gives the best PPG?

There is no universal best location for every task. Signal quality, movement, comfort and sustained wear must be considered together. The wrist is a practical option for everyday monitoring; a clean resting trace alone does not establish which device will perform best during daily life. Explore the location lesson to examine these tradeoffs.

What can you change?

Adjust age, heart rate, arterial stiffness, breathing rate, movement and peripheral perfusion. Compare a single pulse or a live stream. The rhythm examples illustrate irregular timing and amplitude; they are teaching scenarios rather than recordings from a patient.

How do green, red and infrared PPG differ?

Choose green (530 nm), red (660 nm) or infrared (940 nm) and save a reference to compare their illustrative pulse contours at the same location. Green generally samples shallower tissue, while red and infrared sample deeper, overlapping regions. Open Light penetration & simulation science beneath the waveform to explore this visually.

Walking and running introduce baseline shifts, impact spikes and temporary contact loss. In this simulation, red and infrared amplify those artifacts. Research comparing green and infrared during movement informs this scenario, but the exact shapes and noise strengths are teaching assumptions, not measured device performance. Skin, sensor contact, geometry and processing all influence real recordings.

Read the shape of a pulse

The single-beat view labels pulse onset, the systolic peak, the dicrotic notch and the diastolic peak when those features are represented in the model. Capture a reference and compare it with another setting to see changes in timing and contour. Feature visibility varies, so a simulated notch should not be treated as a feature that must appear in every real measurement.

Is this real patient data?

No. Every waveform is generated by an educational model. Plethscape is not a validated digital twin, medical device, diagnostic tool or benchmark of commercial wearables. Device appearances identify sensing locations; they do not demonstrate the performance of those products. Anatomical and signal animations simplify physiology to help explain it.
